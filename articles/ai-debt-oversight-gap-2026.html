Regulators were asked to investigate AI debt eight months ago. Here's what actually happened.

Published September 2026 · 6 min read

In January, four senators asked federal regulators to formally investigate whether AI companies' debt poses a risk to the wider financial system. Treasury never confirmed it would. Here's what happened instead — and why the number that's actually moved since then isn't coming from any regulator.

In January 2026, four U.S. senators sent a formal letter to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent asking the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) to open a real investigation into AI-related debt. Eight months later, here's an honest accounting of what happened: not much, from the regulators. Quite a lot, from the market.

The ask

On January 22, Senator Elizabeth Warren — joined by Senators Richard Blumenthal, Chris Van Hollen, and Tina Smith — asked FSOC to formally investigate whether AI-related borrowing has become a systemic financial-stability risk. The letter wasn't vague hand-wringing; it cited specific numbers: AI companies were on track to spend nearly $3 trillion on data center infrastructure by 2028 while only self-funding about $1.4 trillion of it, leaving a $1.6 trillion gap to be financed through debt markets. It pointed to $400 billion in 2025 AI spending against just $60 billion in revenue, and cited MIT research finding 95% of companies had seen no measurable return on their AI investments so far. It flagged early stress signals already visible in public markets: CoreWeave's valuation had dropped 46% in six weeks, its credit-default-swap costs had surged over 280 basis points, and rating agencies had downgraded Oracle's outlook to "Negative." The senators gave Treasury a deadline — February 13 — to confirm FSOC would use its authority, including compelling data through the Office of Financial Research, to actually investigate.

The non-answer, and the bill that followed

Treasury never confirmed it would pursue the inquiry. So in June, instead of a regulator's report, Warren and Blumenthal introduced the AI Bubble Transparency Act — legislation that would force, by law, the same three things the January letter had asked for voluntarily: require the Office of Financial Research to collect AI-debt exposure data from banks, insurers, and private credit funds; require FSOC to publish a public report assessing how an AI-related shock could spread through the financial system; and require FSOC to recommend specific actions to regulators and Congress. They timed the bill's introduction to land just ahead of a scheduled Senate Banking Committee hearing.

What happened at the hearing

That hearing — titled "AI and the American Dream: Promoting Innovation, Affordability, and American Dominance" — became its own small story. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang had been specifically invited to answer questions about chip exports to China and his company's political donations. He didn't show. Most of the panelists who did appear leaned toward the administration's deregulatory posture; the notable exception was Dr. Sarah Myers West of the AI Now Institute, who testified specifically about national-security and systemic economic risk from under-regulated AI infrastructure spending. The hearing split along predictable lines — Republicans emphasizing innovation and investment, Democrats pressing on financial oversight — and because the Senate is Republican-controlled, the Transparency Act has essentially no near-term path to passage. This was less a bill about to become law and more a marker being placed on the public record.

The market didn't wait for permission

While the formal ask sat unanswered, credit markets kept independently pricing exactly the risk the senators wanted studied. Oracle's five-year credit-default-swap spread — essentially the cost of insuring against Oracle defaulting on its debt — hit a record 212 basis points in July, an 18-year high, up from near zero the year before. Alphabet posted its first negative free-cash-flow quarter since going public in 2004. Moody's, a notably unalarmist voice in this conversation, said credit metrics were "still very strong for most" of the companies carrying this debt, while flagging that "a material shift in the structure of their balance sheets is becoming evident."

What this actually means

Nobody with the legal authority to formally track systemic AI-debt exposure across banks, insurers, and private credit funds is currently doing so — not because the risk wasn't specific enough to act on eight months ago, but because the request landed in a partisan bucket before anyone with the authority to act on it did. In the meantime, the number that's actually moving in real time isn't a regulatory report; it's Oracle's CDS spread, repriced by the bond market every trading day regardless of what Congress does with a bill. If you're trying to track how AI-debt risk is actually developing, credit-default-swap pricing on the most leveraged names in this space is, right now, a more current source than any government dashboard — because there isn't one yet.

That's not a reassuring conclusion. It's just an accurate one: the market is doing the job the senators asked a regulator to do, and it's doing it whether or not anyone acts on what it's showing.
